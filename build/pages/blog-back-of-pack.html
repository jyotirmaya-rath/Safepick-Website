The front of a package is designed by a marketing team. The back is governed by regulations. If you only have a minute in the aisle, spend it on the back. Here is what you will find there on a food sold in Canada, and what each part is for.

1. The nutrition facts table

Most packaged foods in Canada must carry one. It shows the serving size, the calories in that serving, the amounts of 12 nutrients, and a % Daily Value for most of them.

  • Start with the serving size. Everything else in the table is for that amount. If the serving is one cup and you pour two, double every number.
  • Use the % Daily Value. 5% or less is a little. 15% or more is a lot. That one rule works for every nutrient in the table.
  • Decide what you want more of and less of. Usually more fibre, and less sugar, sodium and saturated fat.

In the example, fibre at 14% is close to "a lot", which is good. Sugars at 9% and sodium at 8% sit in the middle.

2. The ingredient list

Ingredients are listed in descending order by weight, so the first ingredient is the one there is most of. If sugar or a refined flour is first, that tells you more than any claim on the front.

Canada also requires every sugar-based ingredient to be grouped together after the word "Sugars". In the example, sugar, honey and molasses appear as one entry, in second place. Listed separately, each might have looked minor.

3. "Contains" and "May contain"

The main food allergens, sources of gluten and added sulphites have to be declared, either in the ingredient list or in a statement beginning "Contains". A "May contain" statement is different: it warns that the allergen is not an ingredient but could be present through cross-contamination. If you or someone you shop for has an allergy, read both, every time. Recipes change.

4. The date

A best-before date tells you how long an unopened product keeps its freshness and quality when stored properly. It is about quality, not a safety guarantee, and it stops applying once the pack is opened. Storage instructions, such as "refrigerate after opening", are usually printed nearby.

5. The barcode

The barcode is the product's identity. The number under the bars, called a GTIN, is unique to that product and pack size. A checkout uses it to find the price. A scanner app uses it to find everything else.

What else you may see

  • The name and address of the company responsible for the product, and the net quantity.
  • Nutrition claims such as "source of fibre" or "low in fat", which are regulated but selective. Check them against the table.
  • The front-of-package symbol. Since January 2026, foods high in saturated fat, sugars or sodium carry a black-and-white magnifying-glass symbol on the front in Canada.
  • In other countries the layout changes but the ideas don't. In India, for example, packs carry an FSSAI licence number and a green or brown mark showing whether the food is vegetarian or non-vegetarian.
