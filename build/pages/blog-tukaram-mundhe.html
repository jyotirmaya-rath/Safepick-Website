Food-safety inspection is not usually the stuff of viral video. In the summer of 2026 it became exactly that in Maharashtra, India, where clips of surprise inspections in restaurant kitchens spread across social media and hotels were reported to be scrubbing their kitchens before the inspectors arrived. The officer behind the drive was Tukaram Mundhe, the state's new Commissioner of the Food and Drug Administration (FDA).

SafePick has no connection to Mr. Mundhe or the Maharashtra FDA. We are writing about him because his story shows something we care about: what happens when the rules about food are taken seriously in public.

From a farm in Beed to the civil service

Tukaram Mundhe was born on 3 June 1975 in Tadsonna, a village in Maharashtra's drought-prone Beed district, into a farming family. Profiles describe a childhood of financial hardship: helping in the fields, selling vegetables at the weekly market and walking to the local district-council school. He went on to study history and political science in Aurangabad, and is reported to have cleared the civil-service examination with an all-India rank of 20, without coaching. He joined the Indian Administrative Service in the 2005 batch, in the Maharashtra cadre.

A career measured in transfers

One number follows Mr. Mundhe everywhere: 25 postings in about 21 years. Indian administrative norms suggest roughly three years in a post. His average has been under one. Supporters see the transfers as the price of refusing to bend. Critics have asked whether his methods were sometimes excessive. Mr. Mundhe has said that transfers gave him opportunities to bring about change.

The food-safety drive

The FDA regulates food, drugs and related products across the state. Under Mr. Mundhe, reports describe statewide action against adulterated milk, fake paneer, unhygienic kitchens, banned gutkha and unsafe medicines, reaching manufacturers, wholesalers, restaurants, dairies and retailers.

According to news reports from the period, the department carried out more than 1,131 inspections, suspended 56 restaurant licences, some of them at well-known establishments, and seized stock worth nearly ₹50 crore. In August 2026 the FDA also issued notices to three film actors over an advertisement that the regulator said indirectly promoted a pan masala product prohibited in Maharashtra.

What made the drive unusual was its visibility. Inspections were filmed and shared, and the commissioner became one of the most recognisable civil servants in the country. Some outlets nicknamed him "Singham", after a film police officer.

The debate over his methods

Not everyone has applauded. The Bombay High Court questioned the FDA's action against an Amazon warehouse, and the department was reported to have withdrawn some orders after the court's criticism. Reports also raised concerns about the workload on FDA staff as inspections multiplied. When rumours of yet another transfer spread, Mr. Mundhe said publicly that he was continuing as commissioner and that the work against unsafe food and spurious medicines would go on.

Both things can be true. Visible enforcement changes behaviour quickly: kitchens get cleaned before the inspector arrives. And enforcement has to stay within the law to last, which is what courts are for.

What shoppers can take from it

  • Food safety is mostly invisible until someone looks. A clean dining room says little about the kitchen, and an attractive pack says little about what is inside.
  • Rules only protect people when they are checked. That is as true of a nutrition label as of a restaurant licence.
  • You can do some of the checking yourself. An inspector can't be in every kitchen, but every shopper can turn a pack over, read the label and ask what is in it.
