"Bad food" is a phrase everyone uses and nobody defines. Nutrition guidance avoids it for a good reason: a slice of birthday cake is not a problem, and a diet of nothing but "good" granola bars can be. What the evidence does point to is narrower and more useful. Packaged foods become a poor everyday choice when they are high in three things.

The three to watch: sugars, sodium and saturated fat

Health Canada singles these out because eating a lot of them, often, is linked to higher risks of heart disease, stroke, high blood pressure and obesity. Since 1 January 2026, most packaged foods sold in Canada that are high in saturated fat, sugars or sodium must say so on the front of the pack, with a black-and-white symbol showing a magnifying glass.

For most products, "high" means a serving supplies 15% or more of the Daily Value for that nutrient. That matches the rule printed at the bottom of every Canadian nutrition table: 5% or less is a little, 15% or more is a lot.

Sugars

The World Health Organization advises keeping free sugars below 10% of daily energy, which is about 50 grams, or 12 level teaspoons, for an adult eating 2,000 calories. A single can of regular pop can use up most of that. Sugars also hide in foods that don't taste like dessert: flavoured yogurt, breakfast cereal, sauces and granola bars.

In Canada, the ingredient list now groups all sugar-based ingredients together after the word "Sugars", so honey, glucose-fructose, molasses and cane sugar can no longer be scattered down the list to look smaller.

Sodium

The WHO's limit is under 2 grams of sodium a day, the amount in about one teaspoon of salt. Very little of what we eat comes from the salt shaker. Most of it is already in the product: instant noodles, soups, chips, deli meats, sauces and many breads.

Saturated and trans fat

The WHO suggests keeping saturated fat under 10% of daily energy and says industrially produced trans fats are not part of a healthy diet and should be avoided. Saturated fat is highest in fatty meats, butter, palm and coconut oil, and the pastries, chocolate and fried snacks made with them.

Three reasons the answer isn't a blacklist

  • Portion and frequency matter more than the food. A food that is high in sugar is a treat, not a poison. The trouble starts when treats become staples.
  • Some nutritious foods are high in one of the three. Cheese is usually high in saturated fat and sodium, yet Health Canada exempts it from the front-of-pack symbol because it is an important source of calcium.
  • "Low fat" or "natural" on the front proves nothing. A low-fat product can be high in sugar. Turn the pack over.

What SafePick does with this

SafePick reads the same numbers you would, for added sugar, total sugar, saturated fat, trans fat and sodium, along with protein, fibre and the ingredient list, and turns them into a score. Products with several concerns get the verdict Avoid, and the page lists the reasons in plain words, such as "high in sodium per 100 g". It describes the label. It never claims to know what a food will do to you. Then it shows similar products that score higher, which is the part that actually changes what goes in the cart.
