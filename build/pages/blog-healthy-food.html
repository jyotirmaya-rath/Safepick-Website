Ask ten people what healthy food is and you will hear ten answers: low fat, low carb, organic, high protein, "natural". Public-health guidance is calmer than that. It doesn't name a miracle ingredient. It describes a pattern, and the pattern fits on one plate.

Start with the plate

Canada's food guide swapped its old serving charts for a single picture: a plate with a glass of water beside it. Half the plate is vegetables and fruits. A quarter is whole grain foods. The last quarter is protein foods. Water is the drink of choice.

You can use the same picture for almost any meal, even when the food is mixed together in a soup, a curry or a stir-fry. If roughly half of what is in the pot is vegetables, you are close.

The pattern in four lines

  • Have plenty of vegetables and fruits. Fresh, frozen and canned can all be good choices.
  • Choose whole grain foods. Oats, brown rice, whole grain bread and pasta.
  • Eat protein foods, and choose the ones that come from plants more often: beans, lentils, nuts, seeds, tofu.
  • Make water your drink of choice.

A few numbers worth knowing

The World Health Organization puts some figures on the same idea. For anyone over ten years old it suggests:

  • at least 400 grams of fruit and vegetables a day;
  • free sugars below 10% of daily energy, and below 5% for more benefit. Free sugars are the ones added to food and drink, plus the sugars in honey, syrups and fruit juice;
  • less than 5 grams of salt a day, about one teaspoon, which is the same as 2 grams of sodium;
  • at least 25 grams of fibre a day from food.

You don't need to weigh anything. These numbers simply explain why the plate looks the way it does: lots of plants, not much added sugar or salt.

Where packaged food fits

The healthiest foods in a store often have no barcode at all: loose vegetables, fruit, dried beans. Most of us also buy bread, yogurt, cereal, sauces and snacks, and that is where a label, or a scanner, earns its place.

Canada's food guide asks people to limit highly processed foods, to use food labels and to be aware of food marketing. In practice that means comparing two similar products and taking the one with more fibre and less sugar, sodium and saturated fat. Health Canada's rule of thumb makes it quick: on the nutrition table, 5% Daily Value or less is a little, and 15% or more is a lot.

Healthy eating is more than the food

The guide also talks about how we eat, not only what. Cook more often. Eat meals with other people. Notice when you are hungry and when you are full. Enjoy your food, including the food of your own culture and traditions. None of that is on a label, and all of it counts.
